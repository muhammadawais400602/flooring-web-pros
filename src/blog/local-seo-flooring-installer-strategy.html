An HVAC company mostly sells one thing in every city. A flooring installer typically sells five or six services (hardwood, carpet, LVP, tile, refinishing, maybe epoxy) across every town they'll drive to. Serve six cities with six services and that's 36 different searches you could show up for.

Many installers squeeze all of that into one page ("We serve the DFW Metroplex") and wonder why they don't rank anywhere. Here's a better way, using Dallas-area towns as an example.

What goes on each landing page

A good service and city page is long enough to be genuinely useful, written for homeowners but structured so Google can understand it. Include:

  1. A clear H1 with the service and city: "Hardwood Floor Installation in Plano, TX"
  2. An opening paragraph that mentions the city naturally
  3. The specific neighborhoods you serve ("from West Plano near Legacy to East Plano")
  4. Photos of real jobs you've completed in that area
  5. Local proof, only if it's true: "We've refinished dozens of oak floors in Plano"
  6. A pricing guide or a clear estimate request button
  7. An embedded Google Map of the area
  8. A few local FAQs, marked up with FAQ schema
  9. Service schema with the city as the area served
  10. Links to your other city pages and to the main page for that service

Not every word has to be unique

Some shared content across pages is normal. Your process, warranty and how estimates work can read much the same on the Plano page and the Dallas page. What matters is that each page also has substantial content that's specific to that city: neighborhoods, local photos, jobs and reviews from there. Google's problem is with thin, copy-pasted pages that add nothing, not with a consistent template.

Spend your writing time on the local sections. That's what makes a page worth ranking.

Internal linking: the step most people skip

Link your pages together in a simple hub-and-spoke pattern:

  • Hub: /hardwood-floor-installation
  • Spokes: /hardwood-floor-installation-dallas, /hardwood-floor-installation-plano and so on

Each city page links back to its hub and across to two or three nearby city pages. This helps Google see how your pages relate and helps visitors find the right one.

Business listings still count

Listings on other trusted sites (often called citations) help Google confirm you're a real local business. Focus on quality over quantity: Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, Yelp, the Better Business Bureau, Nextdoor, Houzz, Angi and your local chamber of commerce. Keep your name, address and phone identical on every one. Hundreds of low-quality directory listings won't do much.

Reviews that mention the city

When customers describe the job and the town in their own words ("They installed hardwood in our Frisco home"), it helps homeowners in that town trust you and gives Google more context. You can't script reviews, but you can ask customers to mention what you did and where. Build that gentle nudge into your review request message. Our guide to getting more 5-star reviews has more on asking the right way.

Keep it honest. Never offer discounts or gifts in exchange for reviews. It's against Google's rules, and incentivised reviews can be removed.

How to measure progress

Local SEO shows progress over months, not days. Check in monthly on:

  • Whether you appear in the Map Pack for your main service and city searches (search from that town, or ask someone local)
  • Google Business Profile performance: searches, calls, website clicks and direction requests
  • Clicks and impressions for each city page in Google Search Console (free)
  • Estimate requests that come from each page
  • What new customers say when you ask how they found you
